Continuous Controls Monitoring: A Practical Setup Guide for ISO 27001, SOC 2, DPDP Act, RBI and CERT-In

By Gayatri Chakrabarty, Blue Copper Technologies · Last updated 20 November 2026

Continuous controls monitoring (CCM) is the automated, scheduled testing of security and compliance controls, with results and evidence stored as they happen. Instead of sampling controls once a year before an audit, CCM checks each control at a set frequency, flags failures to a named owner, and keeps a dated record that auditors can reuse. A practical rollout starts with 15 to 20 high-risk controls that systems can test on their own, and grows from there.

What is continuous controls monitoring?

CCM has three parts:

  • Automated tests: scripts or integrations that check a control’s state (for example, “MFA is enforced for every admin account”) against a pass rule.

  • A schedule: each control runs as often as its risk calls for, from real time to quarterly.

  • An evidence trail: every result is stored with a timestamp, the data it checked and the person who closed any failure.

The idea is older than most compliance tools. NIST described it as Information Security Continuous Monitoring in Special Publication 800-137 in 2011. What has changed is that most controls now live in cloud consoles, identity providers and ticketing systems with APIs, so they can be tested by software.

How is CCM different from continuous compliance?

Continuous compliance is the goal: being audit-ready on any day of the year. CCM is the engine that gets you there. It covers the testing and evidence part of continuous compliance, while policies, risk assessments, training and governance sit around it.

Why do the frameworks expect monitoring now?

Each major framework already asks for ongoing monitoring in its own words:

Framework What it asks for Why point-in-time checks fall short
ISO/IEC 27001:2022 Clause 9.1 requires the organisation to decide what to monitor and measure, how often, and who analyses the results A yearly sample cannot show the “how often” the clause asks you to define
SOC 2 Type II (AICPA Trust Services Criteria) Controls must operate effectively across the whole review period, often 6 to 12 months One failed month can become an exception in the report
India’s DPDP Act and DPDP Rules, 2025 Reasonable security safeguards, logs kept for at least one year, and breach reports to the Data Protection Board with a detailed report within 72 hours You cannot report a breach within 72 hours if you find failed controls months later
CERT-In Directions (28 April 2022) Report specified cyber incidents within 6 hours and keep ICT system logs for 180 days within India Six hours leaves no room for manual detection
RBI directions for regulated entities Ongoing IT governance, risk management and assurance reviews Supervisors expect evidence across the year

Sources: ISO/IEC 27001:2022, DPDP Rules, 2025, CERT-In Directions.

India’s Digital Personal Data Protection Act comes into full force on 13 May 2027. That date is fixed, and every organisation that processes personal data of people in India must be compliant by then.

Controls 17 to 19 are where most global toolkits stop short for India. The DPDP Act ties consent, rights and erasure to evidence, so they belong in the monitored set from day one.

How often should each control be tested?

Set frequency by how fast a failure causes harm and how fast the underlying setting can change:

  • Hourly or real time: settings that can expose data in minutes, such as public storage, logging gaps and alerts from security tools.

  • Daily: identity, patching, endpoints and anything a regulator expects you to report within hours.

  • Weekly or monthly: reviews, vendor documents, training and retention.

  • Quarterly: access recertification, restore tests and policy reviews, with a weekly check that the quarterly task is not overdue.

A useful rule: test a control at least four times inside the window you would have to report a failure in. For a 6-hour CERT-In clock, that means hourly checks on the controls that feed incident detection.

Who should own each control?

Every control needs one named owner who fixes failures, plus a second person who can confirm the fix. Shared ownership (“IT and security”) is the most common reason failures sit open. In our DPDP Act sessions with five leading hospitals in Eastern India, shared logins, contracts without data clauses and data that was never deleted came up again and again. Each of those had no single owner.

What counts as evidence?

Auditors accept automated evidence when it shows four things:

  1. What was tested: the rule and the systems in scope.

  2. When: a timestamp written by the system itself.

  3. The result: pass or fail, with the raw data or a link to it.

  4. What happened next: for a failure, the ticket, the fix and who confirmed it.

Store evidence where it cannot be quietly edited, and map each item to every framework clause it supports. One MFA test result can serve ISO 27001 Annex A 5.17 and 8.5, SOC 2 CC6.1, the DPDP security safeguards rule and RBI access control expectations at once. That reuse is where the time saving comes from.

What are the limits of continuous controls monitoring?

CCM is strong, and it still has blind spots:

  • Not every control can be automated. Board oversight, risk acceptance and policy quality need people. Plan for roughly a third of controls to stay manual, with reminders and attestations.

  • Bad test rules create false comfort. A test that checks “MFA policy exists” passes even when half the users are excluded. Review each rule with the control owner.

  • Alert fatigue is real. Start with fix deadlines that match risk, and suppress repeats until the owner responds.

  • Tools need access. Read-only API access to production systems must be approved and itself monitored.

  • It does not replace the audit. CCM shortens audit preparation. The external auditor still decides.

Frequently asked questions

What is an example of continuous controls monitoring? A daily automated check that every privileged account has MFA enforced. When an account fails, the tool opens a ticket for the IAM lead, records the fix and stores both as audit evidence.

What is the difference between CCM and continuous auditing? CCM is run by management to keep controls working. Continuous auditing is run by internal audit to test those controls independently. Both can use the same data.

How long does it take to implement CCM? A focused rollout of 20 controls takes about 90 days. Adding frameworks later is faster, because most controls are already mapped.

Is CCM required by law? No law names CCM. ISO 27001 clause 9.1, SOC 2 Type II, CERT-In’s 6-hour reporting rule and the DPDP Act’s 72-hour breach report all expect monitoring that a once-a-year check cannot provide.

Does CCM work for small companies? Yes. A smaller company can start with 10 controls and the tools it already uses, such as its identity provider and cloud console.
